Project information

Project title *

GP-RareLink: Literature-Curated, Database-Validated Genotype–Phenotype Records for Rare Disease

What rare disease or disease class does this project focus on? *

Rare diseases showing a measurable gap between the published literature and established databases: OMIM, ClinVar, and Monarch's phenopacket-store. Six are selected for the pilot; all are absent from phenopacket-store's 408 cohorts, and together span four inheritance modes and six organ systems. Alexander disease: GFAP, OMIM 203450, ORPHA 58, AD/de novo, neurology. A 2024 review catalogues 550 patients and 182 causative variants against 72 ClinVar pathogenic/likely-pathogenic and ~12 OMIM allelic variants. Active foundation (United Leukodystrophy Foundation). Leukocyte adhesion deficiency I: ITGB2, OMIM 116920, ORPHA 2968, AR, immunology. 323 published cases with genotype reported for only 139: the clearest gap in the set. A20 haploinsufficiency: TNFAIP3, OMIM 616744, ORPHA 476102, AD, auto-inflammatory. 177 published patients; 542 ClinVar records, only ~58 pathogenic against ~289 VUS. Described in 2016, so the corpus is entirely open-access-era. SSADH deficiency: ALDH5A1, OMIM 271980, ORPHA 22, AR, neurometabolic. ~450 published patients; 143 ClinVar P/LP against 262 VUS, and a proxy count of 5 OMIM allelic variants. Active patient association. Danon disease: LAMP2, OMIM 300257, ORPHA 34587, X-linked dominant, cardiac/neuromuscular. Over 100 published families; 162 ClinVar P/LP against 345 VUS. Sex-differential severity tests whether genotype–phenotype structure survives extraction. Tangier disease: ABCA1, OMIM 205400, ORPHA 31150, AR, lipid metabolism. ~200 published patients; 1,302 ClinVar records but only 69 P/LP, a big gene with a small pathogenic core where the monogenic series must be separated from unrelated testing. Six further verified candidates are held as substitutes should open-access coverage prove thin: Blau syndrome (NOD2), Majeed syndrome (LPIN2), familial LCAT deficiency (LCAT), GAMT deficiency (GAMT), arterial tortuosity syndrome (SLC2A10), PAPA syndrome (PSTPIP1). ClinVar counts via MyVariant.info and ClinVar Miner; OMIM proxy counts are lower bounds, to be replaced with direct counts before work begins.

Problem. Tremendous progress has been made in systematizing rare disease research, but across the large number of diseases and multiple data types the information remains fragmented and scattered, making interpretation difficult. We propose a pilot project providing consolidation and cross-linking for a consolidated view of rare disease — variant information from ClinVar and OMIM, phenotypic information in HPO and partial phenopacket coverage, and the many case reports in the literature. For example, Alexander disease has 182 distinct causative variants across 550 published patients, against 72 ClinVar pathogenic/likely-pathogenic entries and roughly 12 OMIM allelic variants.

Question. Can the SLIDERS method powered by Claude build consolidated variant ↔︎ phenotype ↔︎ disease records from published case reports — at an accuracy we can prove, and at a scale that materially closes this gap?

Methodology.

Extraction. SLIDERS (https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22294) is a novel LLM-based methodology for systematic reviews that automatically assembles evidence tables tailored to extracting structured data from documents, and can extract full-text excerpts serving as direct evidence or as provenance for that data. Core to SLIDERS is an automated evidence reconciliation agent that writes code to analyze and reconcile extracted evidence, bringing together information fragmented across documents, resolving inconsistencies across excerpts, and synthesizing overlapping findings into a coherent evidence table.

We will implement SLIDERS driven by Claude Opus to read case reports and emit GA4GH Phenopackets, which already carry phenotype, genotype, and interpretation — with HGVS normalized through VariantValidator and records checked with CohortValidator.

ClinVar and OMIM as ground truth, not input. We measure concordance on gene, HGVS normalization, classification, and phenotype association against what those databases already assert. This yields a large, automatically-scored accuracy estimate with no adjudicator hours consumed, and it is pre-registered before extraction.

The contribution closing gaps. Variants absent from ClinVar and OMIM are human-adjudicated by the RGI panel, then offered as ClinVar literature-only submissions and OMIM allelic-variant candidates, each carrying phenotype evidence and provenance. Adjudicator hours concentrate here, where they are decisive.

Aggregation. Per gene, we assemble a genotype–phenotype map: which variant classes associate with which phenotype profiles, with denominators and per-publication provenance. Where a gene's ClinVar record is dominated by unrelated testing noise — NOD2 at ~23 pathogenic of 1,055, PSTPIP1 at ~7 of 730 — separating the monogenic allelic series is itself the test.

Ascertainment-bias arm. Against held-out diseases with published natural-history studies, we measure how far literature-derived phenotype frequencies deviate from cohort truth, with a per-disease memorization probe.

Deliverables. We will pilot this on 6 of the diseases outlined above. Outputs: Phenopackets, ClinVar literature-only submissions, OMIM allelic-variant candidates, per-gene genotype–phenotype maps, and the concordance and bias results, published whether favourable or not. We will make the pipeline open source.

Timeline. Month 1: Obtaining the disease literature, OMIM, ClinVar, and related reference information; contacting Monarch and ClinVar maintainers. Month 2: Extraction using SLIDERS with Claude. Month 3: Concordance analysis against ClinVar and OMIM. Month 4: Adjudication of gap variants. Month 5: Prepare submissions, bias analysis. Month 6: Writing up the results for publication and review.

How specifically will Claude's capabilities be used in your research. Please explain how Claude will significantly accelerate or enhance your research compared to existing methods or tools. Please be as detailed as possible about the tasks Claude will perform and how this integrates with your research workflow (200 words max) *

Within SLIDERS, Claude Opus extracts HGVS from non-standard prose, extracts three-state phenotypic features, resolves proband identity across papers via the code-writing reconciliation agent, and binds findings to HPO and Mondo. A second Claude pass audits each claim against its cited span. Claude Code builds the pipeline and concordance harness; Claude Science keeps every number re-runnable.

Claude is chosen for a measured error profile matching provenance-first curation. On DRBench, Claude systems post the two lowest fabricated-citation rates of ten evaluated (3.0% and 3.2%), and Claude's residual errors are stale links to real sources rather than inventions. That is decisive when every extracted value must carry a real PMID and verbatim span. On AA-Omniscience, which rewards declining over guessing, Claude ranks first and third; Opus answers at 46.6% accuracy with 35.9% hallucination where GPT-5 reaches 39% accuracy at 81%. Abstention is a first-class output in our design. On published HPO extraction, Claude Sonnet 4.5 reaches 97.6% precision, which matches the high-precision profile phenopacket curation requires, which matches our own internal experience.

Impact assessment

Please describe the potential scientific impact of your research if successful (200 words max) *

Rare disease information is scattered across databases and literature, and there are far too many diseases to assemble by human annotation. The problem is recurring rather than finite: in routine analysis a team meets a disease nobody has curated, so the need is not to clear a backlog but to point a pipeline at any disease on demand. Building that robustly — connecting genotype to phenotype with provenance a clinician can check — is what this project tests.

The consequence is concrete: a laboratory querying ClinVar today cannot see a causative variant published in 2011 but never submitted, and that family's odyssey continues. Across six diseases we address a counted deficit — roughly 110 unsubmitted variants in Alexander disease alone, and genotypes for 184 leukocyte adhesion deficiency patients whose variants were published but never structured.

This is a proof of concept on two databases and six diseases. If per-disease cost and accuracy hold, the pipeline extends to more sources and thousands of conditions, the marginal cost of each becoming inference rather than curator-years.

Two contributions outlive it: the held-in concordance design, an answer key any literature-extraction group can adopt; and a phenopacket serving diagnosis, natural history, and Matchmaker Exchange without re-curation.

How do you plan to measure the success of using Claude in your research? Please list specific metrics or objectives that would indicate success (100 words max) *

Pre-registered before extraction: positive-control measurement. Concordance: agreement with ClinVar and OMIM on gene, normalized HGVS, classification, and phenotype, with confidence intervals. False positives: under 2% of extracted values unsupported by their cited span, audited on 250 claims; abstention when evidence is thin. Gap closure: variants absent from both databases adjudicated, prepared, and accepted for submission. Bias: per-disease deviation from held-out natural-history cohorts, with memorization probe. Efficiency: expert-minutes per accepted record, and Claude against an open-weight model under identical scaffold. Success is high concordance with a low-false-positive workflow; new database findings are upside.

Additional information

Is there anything else you would like the review committee to know about your application?

What Rare Genomics Institute brings beyond a research partner. RGI is not an academic group that will publish and move on. For fifteen years it has run an operational rare-disease service: 1,200+ families across 33 countries, 410+ sequencing and research projects, and $5.26M in sequencing funded for patients who could not otherwise afford it. In 2016 RGI was named by Illumina as one of the initial partners of the iHope program, providing philanthropic clinical whole-genome sequencing to undiagnosed children in financial need — work that continues today.

Three assets follow from that history, and they are why this work will not stay academic.

A consented cohort. RGI Patient Research Services holds whole-genome and exome data paired with de-identified medical records, individually consented for research. This pilot deliberately does not touch it — we work only from published literature, so no human-subjects determination sits on the critical path — but that cohort is the natural validation set for what follows, and it means the method has somewhere real to go the moment the pilot proves out.

A patient community at scale. RGI operates RareShare, with over 10,000 members across more than 1,000 rare-disease communities. Few groups building genotype–phenotype resources can put a draft record in front of people who actually live with the condition.

Patient voice, not just patient data. RGI's advocacy team assigns every family a dedicated advocate from first contact through program completion. The organisation is accountable to the people whose case reports this project reads. That accountability shapes the design: provenance on every claim, human sign-off before any submission, and results published whether favourable or not — because the people affected are the ones who bear the cost of a confidently wrong answer.

In addition, Rare Genomics and the Stanford OVAL lab has already been working together for the past year creating two tools: AutoOMIM (generating OMIM entries from literature) and OpenRare (using STORM to create rare disease Wikipedia-like entries). We are excited to expand our partnership with the Claude grant.
